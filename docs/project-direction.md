# Project direction

Cognitive Biases is no longer centred on the mobile app. The website and the knowledge library are the product.

## What we are building

A clear, useful and regularly reviewed guide to human cognitive biases, systematic AI behaviour and the decision patterns that appear when people and AI systems interact.

The project should help a person answer five simple questions:

1. What is this pattern?
2. What do we actually know about it?
3. Where can it matter in real decisions or AI systems?
4. What is it often confused with?
5. What can I test before I trust or act?

The same reviewed material should also be available as structured data so search tools, assistants and agents can use it without scraping pages or guessing what a page means.

## Three connected layers

### 1. Human Biases

Psychological constructs, evidence, examples and practical decision tools for people. These pages keep the existing human-centred cognitive-bias library as a major pillar.

### 2. AI Systematic Biases

Repeatable directional sensitivities and evaluation distortions observed in language-model systems. Model-specific claims are dated evidence snapshots. A finding from one checkpoint, provider or product surface does not automatically apply to another.

Human cognitive-bias names may be used as analogies when a controlled experiment justifies the comparison. The project must not imply that a model has the same mental mechanism or subjective psychology as a person.

The AI layer should be useful for chat assistants, coding and automation agents, RAG/document systems, research assistants, LLM-as-a-judge evaluation and decision-support workflows.

### 3. Human–AI Interaction Patterns

Patterns that emerge from people, models and information environments acting together. The existing Observatory and AI-era research tracks belong here when the unit of analysis is the interaction rather than the model alone.

Do not collapse these three layers just because they share familiar words such as anchoring, authority or confirmation.

## What makes the project different

We are not trying to win by having the longest list of bias names. Many websites already have lists.

We want to be useful because we keep the material connected, testable and reviewed:

- plain explanations for people;
- evidence notes that say when a claim is strong, limited, mixed or still unreviewed;
- dated model snapshots instead of timeless claims about AI providers;
- practical self-tests for important AI failure modes;
- comparisons between ideas that are easy to confuse;
- decision contexts such as forecasting, product work and decisions made with AI;
- system contexts such as chat, agents, RAG and LLM-as-a-judge;
- sources and review dates;
- reusable public data;
- research notes that explain what changed and why it matters.

## Writing style

Write for an interested person, not for a search engine and not for an AI model.

Prefer short sentences and ordinary words. Explain specialist terms when they are needed. Do not decorate simple ideas with technical language.

Use phrases such as "the research we reviewed suggests", "we found", "the evidence is mixed" and "we do not know yet" when they are accurate. The project should distinguish what we know from what we infer.

Do not present a cognitive bias as a diagnosis or as a label for another person. Do not describe an AI system as having human psychology when the evidence only shows a behavioural output pattern.

## Product principles

### Evidence before confidence

A memorable claim is not automatically a reliable claim. If the literature is disputed or narrow, the page should say so.

For AI behaviour, prefer peer-reviewed controlled multi-model studies, transparent benchmarks and provider incident reports. Preprints may enter the research queue, but publication in the public catalog requires editorial review of method and scope.

### Fresh model claims

AI behaviour changes faster than most psychological evidence. Preserve older findings as historical snapshots and make recency visible. Retest after material model, post-training, system-prompt or product-surface changes instead of silently carrying an old label forward.

### Original writing

Sources are used to understand the subject, not as text to copy. New editorial content must be written in our own words and linked to the sources that support it.

### Useful before comprehensive

A smaller reviewed section is better than a large section filled with weak claims. Legacy entries can remain available while they are gradually reviewed.

### Human first, machine readable second

Public pages should make sense to a person without knowing the data model. Machine-readable files may be technical, but they must describe the same reviewed knowledge rather than a separate version of the truth.

### One source of truth

Names, relationships, evidence status and sources belong to the canonical record. Language versions should translate the reviewed meaning instead of creating independent copies.

## Research and freshness loop

The general research scout continues to collect candidate cognitive-bias research. A dedicated AI-bias scout searches recent work on sycophancy, cognitive-bias analogues, LLM evaluation bias, option/context position effects, RAG source conflict and AI summarization integrity.

The scout may add candidates to a research inbox. It must not automatically turn a candidate paper into a canonical public claim. Promotion requires source review, evidence grading and a dated model snapshot.

Model-specific snapshots enter a fast review window after 90 days. The default catalog review interval is 180 days. A major new model generation or evaluation method can trigger earlier review.

## Near-term focus

1. Keep the website and knowledge base as the main product.
2. Make Human Biases and AI Systematic Biases clearly discoverable as two major public pillars, with Human–AI Interaction Patterns as the bridge.
3. Expand the AI catalog only when evidence is strong enough to describe the effect and its scope.
4. Turn high-value AI entries into reproducible self-tests and benchmark cases where practical.
5. Continue evidence review of high-value human entries.
6. Publish clear Research, Data and Partnerships pages.
7. Build public data releases from the same records used by the site.
8. Run research workflows that collect candidate papers and updates, check them, and propose changes without publishing unsupported claims automatically.
9. Keep English canonical and add German and Russian as reviewed language layers.

## Success measures

Do not measure the project mainly by the number of bias names.

Track instead:

- reviewed human concepts;
- AI bias entries with recent evidence snapshots;
- stale AI snapshots identified and retested;
- reproducible AI self-tests and benchmark runs;
- concepts with reliable sources;
- useful comparisons and decision/system contexts;
- research notes published;
- data downloads and reuse by agents or other projects;
- references or integrations by other projects;
- search visits to reviewed topic pages;
- corrections made when better evidence appears.
